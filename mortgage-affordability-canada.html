Mortgage affordability in Canada

What a lender will let you borrow, what you can actually afford, and what it costs to close — the rules, the maths, and a worked example.

Mortgage affordability is a simple question with a fiddly answer: how much house can you afford? A mortgage affordability calculator in Canada works it out from four numbers — your income, your other debts, your down payment, and your interest rate — and then runs them through the rules lenders have to follow. This guide explains those rules in plain English, so you know what the calculator is doing and why its number is what it is.

Two numbers matter, and they are not the same. The first is the most a lender would likely let you borrow. The second is the price that is actually comfortable for you. The calculator shows both: the maximum, and a comfortable price 12% below it. Everything below uses the same example all through — a household earning $100,000 a year, with $500 a month in other debts, $50,000 saved, a 5% rate and a 25-year amortization, buying in BC as a first-time buyer. The calculator says they can afford up to $422,815.

What decides how much house you can afford?

Four things. Your gross household income, before tax — both incomes together if you are buying as a couple. Your other monthly debts: car loans, credit cards, student loans and lines of credit, at their minimum payments. Your down payment — what you have saved. And your mortgage rate. Change any one of them and the answer moves. That is the point of the calculator: type in your own numbers and the answer updates as you type.

But income alone does not set the price. Lenders run your numbers through two tests — the stress test, and the GDS and TDS limits — and the smallest answer wins. So the question is not just how much mortgage can I afford on my salary, it is how much a lender will let me borrow once those tests are applied.

The mortgage stress test

Your lender does not qualify you at the rate they offer. They qualify you at the higher of your rate plus 2%, or 5.25%. So a 5% mortgage means you must prove you could pay at 7%. That is the rule for every insured mortgage in Canada, and most lenders use it for uninsured ones too. A mortgage stress test calculator does exactly this: it works out the payment at the higher qualifying rate, not the rate on the offer.

It is the reason the maximum is lower than many people expect — and it protects you if rates rise. In the worked example, the 5% rate becomes a 7% qualifying rate, and the payment the budget allows is $2,717 a month at that 7% rate, even though the real payment at 5% is $2,247 a month.

GDS and TDS: the 39% and 44% limits

Two ratios decide how much housing cost you can carry. GDS — gross debt service — is your housing costs (mortgage payment at the stress-test rate, property tax, heating, and half of any condo fees) divided by your gross monthly income. It must be no more than 39%. TDS — total debt service — is the same housing costs plus all your other monthly debt payments. It must be no more than 44%.

The calculator works out both and uses whichever gives the smaller number. In the example, the monthly housing budget is $3,167, and TDS is what limits the price — because of the $500 a month in other debts. Pay off a debt and the number goes up.

How much down payment do I need?

The minimum down payment in Canada is 5% of the first $500,000 of the price, plus 10% of the part above $500,000. So a $400,000 home needs at least $20,000 down, and a $750,000 home needs at least $50,000. On a home priced at $1 million or more the minimum jumps to 20%, so a $1 million home needs $200,000 down. If you put down less than 20%, your mortgage also needs CMHC insurance, which is added to the mortgage.

The minimum is not the whole story. A bigger down payment lowers your mortgage, and it can drop you into a cheaper insurance tier — going from just under 10% down to 10% cuts the CMHC premium rate from 4.00% to 3.10%. The calculator page has the full detail, and the calculator itself will show you what your savings can buy.

What is the CMHC premium?

If your down payment is under 20% of the price, your mortgage must have default insurance — usually called CMHC insurance, though Sagen and Canada Guaranty sell the same thing at the same rates. It protects the lender, not you: if you can't pay, the insurer covers the lender. The CMHC premium is a percentage of your mortgage: 4.00% with 5% to 9.99% down, 3.10% with 10% to 14.99% down, and 2.80% with 15% to 19.99% down. Amortizations over 25 years add 0.20%. The premium is normally added to your mortgage, so you pay interest on it too. With 20% or more down there is no premium at all, and insured mortgages are only available on homes under $1.5 million.

In the worked example, the $422,815 home with $50,000 down (11.83%) has a premium of 3.10%, which is $11,557 added to the mortgage. The tiers matter: going from just under 10% down to 10% drops the rate from 4.00% to 3.10% — on a $400,000 mortgage that is $16,000 versus $12,400, a $3,600 difference. One more thing to budget for: Ontario charges 8% tax on the premium, paid in cash on closing day, and Saskatchewan and Quebec charge tax on it too.

Land transfer tax in every province

The land transfer tax calculator on each province page works out the tax on any price. The rules are different everywhere: BC, Ontario, Manitoba, Quebec, New Brunswick, Nova Scotia and Prince Edward Island charge a tax on the price; Alberta, Saskatchewan, Newfoundland and Labrador and the three territories charge registration fees instead. First-time buyers pay less in BC, Ontario and PEI, and get a tax credit back later in Quebec. A first time home buyer in BC can pay no tax at all on a home up to $500,000.

Cash to close: the closing costs

The down payment is not the whole bill. On closing day you also need the land transfer tax (after any first-time buyer relief), about $1,500 in legal fees, and the CMHC premium if you have one. That total is your cash to close, and it is the number most buyers forget to budget for. A closing costs calculator built for Canada adds all of it up — the calculator on this site does, and it also shows the smaller out-of-pocket number for the usual case, where the premium is rolled into the mortgage instead of paid in cash.

In the worked example: $50,000 down payment, $0 land transfer tax after the exemption, about $1,500 in legal fees, and the $11,557 premium — $63,057 in total, or $51,500 out of pocket if the premium goes on the mortgage.
